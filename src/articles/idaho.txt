Idaho falls

In the four years since the Supreme Court overturned Roe v. Wade, prompting a near-total ban on Idaho abortions, more than a third of the state’s obstetrician-gynecologists have left, closed their offices, stopped practicing obstetrics or retired. Now half of Idaho’s counties don’t have an OB-GYN. There are just 170 obstetricians in the state today — less than two for every 10,000 women there. (Nationally, it’s about twice that.)

Facts from the Gem State: Three rural hospitals have closed their labor and delivery wards because they don’t have enough doctors. The wait time for a hysterectomy, even in more populated areas like Boise, can be six months. For an annual exam, it’s more like nine months. And if women face life-threatening complications during childbirth or a miscarriage, they’re increasingly flown out of state for treatment that OB-GYNs in Idaho could be jailed for administering.

The result? It’s now more dangerous to be pregnant in Idaho. My colleague Lisa Lerer spent a year interviewing doctors, public health officials, patients and reproductive rights activists across the state to learn how doctors are coping. “In a way, they are like pioneer doctors, working at the frontier of a new America where abortion laws limit the care they can offer,” Lisa writes.

That could change this year. A referendum on the ballot would restore abortion rights until what’s called “fetal viability,” which was the standard that Roe set. But the loss of all those doctors will be felt for years.

In danger

Lisa provides abundant examples of the peril.

One new mother was hemorrhaging after she gave birth. It’s a common cause of severe complications. A patient can bleed to death in less than 10 minutes. Her family-practice doctors rushed to stanch the bleeding. If they failed to do so, the patient would need surgery to remove her uterus — a hysterectomy. It is the second most common surgery for women in the United States. (A cesarean section is first.) Hundreds of thousands of hysterectomies are performed each year.

In Idaho today, though, a hysterectomy can be a problem. One of the bleeding patient’s doctors had a specialty in high-risk obstetrics. She could deliver babies, implant IUDs, conduct gynecological exams and even perform minor operations to remove fetal tissue after a miscarriage to prevent infection. But she wasn’t trained for hysterectomies. She wasn’t an OB-GYN. And on this day, there were no OB-GYNs available to help.

She thought through her options with her colleagues. If the bleeding didn’t stop, they decided, they’d ask the general surgeon on call for the hospital for assistance. This wasn’t a great safety net. General surgeons don’t perform hysterectomies as regularly as OB-GYNs. It’s not really in their wheelhouse, the family doctor told Lisa. But “your other option is the patient dies,” the doctor said.

DARK NUTRITION

I once ate a full lobe of a cow’s brain at a diner in St. Louis. I’ve eaten pounds of strange-adjacent proteins: raccoon, squirrel, ptarmigan. But I’ve got nothing on the “dark nutrition” crowd experimenting with all sorts of unusual diets. The Times spoke with many of them: eaters of raw beef, chicken and roadkill; disavowers of seed oils and vegetables; consumers of colostrum, butter and egg yolks.

Some swear by organ meats, others by cane sugar or raw milk. You can choose your own adventure. Because dark nutrition isn’t a specific diet. Instead, it’s a style of thinking about food and health. And its central tenet is: Most of what you think you know about food is wrong. Come meet the true believers. Among other things, you’ll learn about meat cereal.

CRUZ CONTROL

Penélope Cruz has spent 30 years masterminding one of the most versatile careers in Hollywood history. Marcela Valdes profiled the actress for The Times and learned a great deal about her craft.
