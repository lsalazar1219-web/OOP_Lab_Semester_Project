Eighteen people have been killed and 27 others wounded in a series of shootings — including four mass shootings involving four or more victims — across Baltimore over the past five weeks, raising questions about whether the city can sustain the recent reductions in violence and what it will take to stop the renewed bloodshed.

Just this month, the deadliest of the year so far, Baltimore has recorded 18 homicides through Tuesday afternoon, twice the nine reported during the entire month of October 2025, with 10 days still remaining. The number of nonfatal shootings citywide this year has also surpassed the total recorded at this point last year.

You may see some outlets reporting 17 homicides for October. The discrepancy comes in the case of Claudia Bismark. The 76-year-old woman died after a violent robbery and assault on Fleet Street. Bismark passed away in October but the incident happened in September, so some agencies add that case to the September numbers instead.

The recent violence has narrowed Baltimore’s year-to-date gains. As of Tuesday morning, police had recorded 101 homicides, compared with 105 at the same point last year. Nonfatal shootings, meanwhile, have surpassed last year’s pace, with 253 reported so far this year compared with 247 in 2025.

At the beginning of October, homicides were down 15.3% and nonfatal shootings were down 4.2% from the same period last year. Those figures now stand at a 3.8% decrease in homicides and a 2.4% increase in nonfatal shootings.

The Mayor’s Office of Neighborhood Safety and Engagement called the shootings “incredibly concerning” Tuesday, pointing to the proliferation of illegal firearms and conflicts escalating into violence.

“These incidents underscore that there is more work that needs to be done collectively to intervene in and prevent violence, hold violent offenders accountable, and co-produce public safety with and for our residents,” Jack French, a spokesperson for the agency, said in an email.

In a joint response to questions from The Baltimore Sun, the city and the Baltimore Police Department said they are “closely monitoring” crime data and emerging trends so that they can adjust their strategies as needed.

Police officials said they are also examining the circumstances surrounding each mass shooting. Officials said some stemmed from internal disputes or retaliation but did not identify a common cause for the recent series of shootings.

“Regardless of the circumstances, the reckless use of firearms puts entire communities at risk, including innocent bystanders,” the city and police said.

A deadly month

The most recent mass shooting occurred Sunday night at a gas station in the 2800 block of Edmondson Avenue, where five people were shot. A 20-year-old man and a 34-year-old woman died from their injuries. Three other victims were wounded.

Three days earlier, five people, including a 14-year-old and a 15-year-old, were shot in the Baltimore Highlands neighborhood of Southeast Baltimore. Erick Juarez-Aviles, 36, and Juan Espinoza-Trampa, 41, were killed.

On Oct. 9, 19-year-old Wesley Harris and 18-year-old Donte Gregory were killed and two others wounded in a shooting in the 1200 block of Oakhurst Place in West Baltimore.

Police have not announced any suspects in the three mass shootings that occurred on Oct. 9, 15 and 18.

The violence also claimed the life of 12-year-old Khloe Ishway, who was killed Oct. 3 during a youth football game at Leon Day Park. Three other adults were wounded, along with the man police have charged in her killing, 23-year-old Tyree Reynolds. Police say he exchanged words with two men before pulling a short-barreled rifle from his backpack and firing toward the football field, drawing return fire from one of the other men.

The earliest of the five mass shootings occurred Sept. 16, when seven people, including a 3-year-old boy, were wounded in the 200 block of North Caroline Street at East Fayette Street. Police have not announced any suspects in that shooting.

Together, the five incidents have left 26 people shot, including Reynolds, who is among the 19 wounded.

Reasons for the violence

City officials and public health experts say the recent shootings underscore the difficulty of preventing interpersonal disputes from escalating into deadly violence.

French said MONSE believes the shootings are connected to conflicts between groups, made more dangerous by the availability of illegal firearms, assault weapons and devices that can convert firearms to automatic fire.

“The continued proliferation of illegal firearms, assault weapons, and automatic sears on our streets combined with the failure for people to be able to resolve their conflicts peacefully has meant that even the smallest dispute has the potential to turn into a mass casualty incident,” French said.

Woodie Kessel, a professor at the University of Maryland, College Park, School of Public Health, said there is no single explanation for mass shootings. Economic hardship, access to firearms and tensions within communities can all contribute to gun violence, he said.

“Unfortunately, it’s multifactorial and complex,” said Kessel, who is co-director of the university’s Prevent Gun Violence: Research, Empowerment, Strategies & Solutions initiative.

Kessel said struggles with employment and the costs of necessities such as food and gasoline can contribute to pressures within communities. But understanding a particular outbreak of violence requires examining the circumstances in the neighborhoods where shootings occur.

“We don’t have a specific, straightforward explanation for all of it,” he said.

The recent shootings represent a troubling shift after years of substantial reductions in Baltimore’s gun violence. The city’s homicide total fell to its lowest level in nearly 50 years in 2025, while nonfatal shootings also declined significantly.

City’s response

French said MONSE has activated or is in the process of activating a Coordinated Neighborhood Stabilization Response in each area affected by the mass shootings.

The response includes checking in with residents, distributing contact information and connecting people with resources following violent incidents.

French said the agency is continuing to work with police and community violence intervention partners to prevent further violence, change attitudes toward the use of violence and stabilize neighborhoods following traumatic events.

In their joint statement, the city and police said additional resources are being directed toward investigating the shootings, recovering illegal firearms and preventing further violence, including possible retaliatory attacks.

The department is also working with community partners to encourage people to resolve conflicts without resorting to gunfire, officials said.

“We will continue to direct enforcement and investigative resources toward the people and places driving violence, strengthen partnerships to disrupt ongoing conflicts, recover illegal firearms, and support community-based violence prevention efforts,” the city and police said.
